How to draw a rangoli by hand
  1. Put down the dots in a square grid. Here the grid is turned on its corner, like a diamond.
  2. Start your line between two dots, never on one.
  3. Curve around a dot, then slip through the gap between two dots, always diagonally.
  4. When you reach the edge, or a turning point, swing back around the dot instead of going on.
  5. Where the line crosses itself, go over and under in turn, like weaving a basket.
  6. Keep going without lifting your hand until you are back where you started. Every dot ends up inside its own little loop.

Tip: turn on “Turning points” to see where the line has to turn back. Slide “Moment” to go through the drawing slowly.

The parts of a sine wave

A sine wave is the height of a point going round a circle, drawn over time. Every part of the wave is a part of the circle. Move one slider at a time and watch which part changes.

θ, ω and t

t is the time in seconds. θ (theta) is the angle of the arm, measured anticlockwise from the dashed line pointing right. The arm turns ω radians every second, starting from φ, so θ = ω · t + φ. The height is the offset plus A · sin θ, and that height, drawn over time, is the wave.

Change one part at a time

Hz (hertz) means “per second”. With a phase other than 0°, a grey dashed wave shows the same wave with φ = 0°, so you can see how far it slid.

Building a square wave from sine waves

1. Start with one sine wave

It has the same period as the square wave (the yellow dashed line), so it goes up and down at the right times, but its corners are round.

2. Add a wave 3 times as fast and ⅓ as tall

It pushes the middle of each flat part down and the edges up: the shape gets flatter and steeper.

3. Keep adding: 5 times as fast and ⅕ as tall, 7 and ⅐ …

Only odd speeds (1, 3, 5, 7 …), each one as tall as 1 divided by its speed. Every new wave is smaller and faster, and fixes smaller details.

4. Never quite perfect

The gap shrinks with every wave, but right next to each jump the sum always overshoots by about 9%. This is called the Gibbs phenomenon.

5. Other shapes, other recipes

A triangle wave also uses odd speeds, but shrinks much faster (1 ÷ speed², every other one upside down), so a few waves are enough. A sawtooth uses every speed.
