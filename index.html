A kolam is a line rangoli from South India: one closed line that loops around a grid of dots without ever touching them. Watch how each one is drawn, then let circles draw it.

More crossings: the line weaves through the gaps more often. Fewer: it curls around single dots more often.

The recipe: circle size for every speed

Each bar is one circle. Colours group the speeds by their remainder when divided by 4. Heights use a square-root scale, so small circles still show. Hover or tap a bar for its numbers. Try switching between “Turn 4 ways” and “Free”.

How to draw a kolam by hand
  1. Put down the dots in a square grid. Here the grid is turned on its corner, like a diamond.
  2. Start your line between two dots, never on one.
  3. Curve around a dot, then slip through the gap between two dots, always diagonally.
  4. When you reach the edge, or a turning point, swing back around the dot instead of going on.
  5. Where the line crosses itself, go over and under in turn, like weaving a basket.
  6. Keep going without lifting your hand until you are back where you started. Every dot ends up inside its own little loop.

Tip: turn on “Turning points” to see where the line has to turn back. Slide “Moment” to go through the drawing slowly.

What are θ, ω and t?

1. θ (theta): where the point is

θ is the angle between the arm and the dashed line pointing right, measured anticlockwise. A full turn is 360°, which mathematicians write as 2π radians (about 6.28).

2. t: time

t counts the seconds since the start. Watch it tick in “Right now”. Reset time starts it again at 0.

3. ω (omega): how fast it turns

ω is the angle the arm turns every second, in radians per second. ω = 6.28 (that is 2π) means one full turn per second; ω = 3.14 means half a turn. A minus ω turns the other way.

4. Together: θ = ω · t + φ

After t seconds the arm has turned ω · t. φ (phi) is the angle it started at, when t was 0.

5. The height draws a wave

The height of the point is r · sin θ (its sideways position is r · cos θ). Plot that height over time and you get a sine wave. A longer arm r makes a taller wave. A bigger ω packs more waves into each second: ω ÷ 2π waves per second.

6. Add circles, add waves

Put a second circle on the tip of the first and their heights add up, so their waves add up. With the right circles you can build almost any repeating wave. Try “Square wave”: speeds 1, 3, 5 and 7 times the first circle, radii 1, ⅓, ⅕ and ⅐ of it. That is a Fourier series, the same idea that draws your pictures in modes 1 to 3.
